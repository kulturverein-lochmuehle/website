## Rendering it

The model renders through `HousesScene`: hand it a canvas and the element its
colours are read from, size it, point it, render. It renders on demand -
nothing in it moves, so a render loop would only warm the device.

```ts
import { HousesScene } from '@kvlm/visualization';

const scene = new HousesScene(canvas, host);
scene.resize(canvas.clientWidth, canvas.clientHeight);
scene.setView({ azimuth: 120, elevation: 45, span: 420 });
scene.render();
```

The camera is orthographic and looks at the mill from the south west by default.
`azimuth` is the compass direction it looks from, `elevation` its angle above
the horizon, `span` the width of the visible ground in meters. A small span is
a close look at the yard, a large one takes in the whole valley.

## Baked views

What the site shows is not the scene above but views of it baked beforehand
(`VIEWS` in `bake/views.ts`, `bakeView()` in `bake/prefab.ts`), each of one of
two kinds:

- **still** - an eye on the plan, how high over the ground, where it looks and
  how wide. Only the triangles that eye can see are kept: within its width and
  depth, facing it, and not wholly behind others.
- **free** - the whole model, uncut, to be looked at from anywhere; the eye is
  only where it starts. Faces drawn on both sides become a face each side.

Either way the model is lit as the scene lights it and the light baked into
the colours, merged by how it is drawn, and written to `views/<name>.view.bin`,
with a list of all of them in `views/index.json`. A view's name is its file's
and its page's on the site's demo, `/demo/<name>` - one name everywhere:
the day it was set up, what it looks at, and its kind
(`20260926-street-still`, `20260926-whole-free`).

```sh
npm run data:views
```

The test suite says when a view is stale, as it does for the terrain.

## Working on the model

The terrain, the crossings and the seam that joins them have a document of
their own: see [SEAM.md](./SEAM.md). The tools that work on them - the seam's
handles, the landfill brush, the reference points, moving the camera - are the
editor's, in `packages/editor`.

## Where the scene comes from

The geometry is not modelled by hand. `data.ts` is generated by
`scripts/fetch-data.ts` (`bun run data:fetch`) from two sources:

- **OpenStreetMap** via Overpass - the footprints of the three buildings, the
  Lotzebach (joined across the culverts OSM splits it at), the two roads that
  say something about the place, and the outline of the woodland.
- **DGM1** via the height WMS of the Landesamt für Geobasisinformation Sachsen -
  the terrain of the Lotzebachtal from the one meter laser scan grid, sampled
  every 10m across a 640m square. The 25m models average the valley away and put
  the mill thirty meters up a slope it stands below.

Built from that at runtime, in three.js:

- The **terrain** is a radial mesh around the yard, smooth shaded and at true
  scale - the DGM1 carries the valley on its own. Everything standing on it
  is flat shaded, which is where the low-poly look lives.
- The **yard** is flat. The ground is levelled around the walls of the
  buildings and takes up the real slope within a few meters of them, so the
  valley wall stays as close and as steep behind the mill as it is.
- The **buildings** are extruded from their footprints. OpenStreetMap carries no
  heights for them, so eaves, ridge and storeys live with the house they belong
  to - one `*.building.ts` each, shaped by `profile.ts` and drawn
  by `buildings.ts` - checked against the orthophoto of the site.
- The **woods** are two instanced meshes, trunk and crown, scattered
  deterministically inside the woodland outline and kept off the yard and ways.
  Their heights and crown sizes are measured: the survey's surface model minus
  its terrain model gives a canopy of 20m in the middle, 30m at the ninth tenth
  and 37m at the tallest, with crowns about ten meters across. The spacing is
  the measured one thinned by half, or the mill would be under the canopy.
- **Roads are built, not painted.** Their lines are rounded first - OSM traces a
  bend as straight pieces - and each one then holds the gradient of its own
  averaged profile, is level across its width, and the terrain beside it is cut
  down or filled up to meet it within a shoulder's width. Brook and roads read
  their height off the terrain mesh, not off the height field, and are stacked
  by layer: road over lane, lane over brook, all of them over the ground.

Nothing casts a shadow. A few thousand trees and three buildings cost more to
shadow than the shadows are worth at this scale.

Without WebGL the renderer cannot be made, and `new HousesScene()` throws;
the editor's viewport catches that and fires `kvlm-editor-failed`.
